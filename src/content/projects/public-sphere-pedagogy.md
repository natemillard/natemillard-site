Most coursework is written for one reader, who is paid to read it, and who is
the only person it will ever reach. Students know this. It is why so much of
what they hand in is competent and inert.

Public Sphere Pedagogy changes the audience. Students research a real problem,
usually one on their own campus, and then present the case in a public forum to
faculty, staff, community members, and anyone else who turns up — people with
no obligation to be generous. Chico was the university where this started, and
for eight years I designed courses and ran events around it.

The clearest example is the toilets. A student team looked at water use in
campus buildings, costed a retrofit, and argued it in front of a room. They
were awarded funding. Then they installed the fixtures themselves. The two
photographs below are the same project a few months apart: the poster, and the
wrench. Everything I believe about this way of teaching is in the distance
between those two pictures.

The structural version of the same idea is the U-Course, which sits inside the
[First-Year Experience](/work/first-year-experience) work. Two general
education courses were merged into a single project-based course and taught in
a room designed and built for it, with peer mentors embedded in the room rather
than bolted on afterwards — near-peers modelling how you behave when you do not
yet know what you are doing. First-year students are usually asked to sit in
rows and receive. This asked them to make something and defend it, in their
first year, in front of strangers.

It runs into the sustainability work as well. [This Way to
Sustainability](/work/sustainability-studies-pathway) was a public forum of the
same kind at a larger scale, where student projects met an audience from
outside the university.

I did not think of any of this as preparation for community work at the time.
It plainly was. The conviction underneath it — that people own what they have
had to stand up and say out loud, and that an audience with no obligation to be
kind is the thing that makes the work real — is the same conviction I now bring
to rooms of adults who are not students, deciding what their own community
should do.
