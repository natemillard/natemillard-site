First-year programmes usually fail in the same place: they treat belonging as
an outcome to be produced rather than a condition students either find or
don't. You cannot bolt belonging onto a lecture. It comes from what students
are asked to do, who they do it with, and whether anyone outside the room ever
sees the result.

So most of the work was structural. With faculty we built the U-Course, merging
two general education courses into a single project-based course, taught in a
room designed and built for the format rather than in whatever lecture hall was
free. Student teams researched real problems and presented the work publicly,
which is the [Public Sphere Pedagogy](/work/public-sphere-pedagogy) side of it.
The photograph above is that room, full.

Peer mentors were embedded in the course rather than offered alongside it. That
distinction is the whole thing. A mentor sitting in the room is a near-peer
model: a first-year student watches someone barely older than them not know
something, ask about it, and carry on. That is social learning, and it is more
persuasive than any amount of advice delivered at a scheduled appointment.

Which meant a large share of the effort went into the mentors themselves —
structured coaching, reflection, and real leadership practice, on the theory
that a well-supported nineteen-year-old is more use to a frightened
eighteen-year-old than any programme design. They were closer to the experience
than any of us and were mostly under-trained for what we were asking of them.

I taught into these courses as well, on the social construction of identity and
how group participation shapes it, which is the same question I now ask about
communities rather than classrooms.
