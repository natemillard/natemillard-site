There is a particular kind of preparedness poster that has been hanging in
American public buildings for forty years. It shows a plastic tub. Inside the
tub are bottled water, canned food, a flashlight, a battery radio, and a first
aid kit. The message beneath it says: *be ready*.

I have no argument with the tub. The tub is fine. My argument is with what the
tub quietly assumes — that you have somewhere to store it, that you will still
be in that place when the thing happens, and that the gap between you and
safety is measured in supplies rather than in relationships.

## The household is not the unit

Preparedness campaigns address the household because the household is easy to
address. It has a mailing address and a television. But the research on who
actually survives disasters well keeps pointing somewhere else: to whether
people know their neighbours, whether someone notices when they do not come
outside, whether there is a person who will drive them.

## What this would change

If the unit of preparedness is the relationship rather than the household, then
the intervention is not a tub. It is time spent in a room together, which is
slower, harder to fund, and much harder to photograph.
