Life and Death in El Paredon Buena Vista, Guatemala

From a deep early sleep, I heard Yolli call to me, “Nate.” She said something in Spanish, but my brain was not ready to translate; however, I knew what she needed. They asked me, before I went to sleep, if there were complications with the pregnancy, could I drive them to the hospital. Here, in this remote village on the Pacific Ocean, running a surf hotel, we had the only vehicle for use. Of course, I told them to wake me.

In the fuzzy, half-head of broken sleep, I responded, “Necesita ir a la hospital?” I wondered, for a second, if hospital was feminine of masculine. I use the feminine for everything.

“Si.”

I got myself up, pulled on shorts, grabbed the keys, and walked around the hotel to the truck. I opened the gate, and stood for a second. Nobody was there. The night was quiet except for the breaking waves thundering into the dark. I stood there, wondering if I dreamed the whole thing. I could see Yolli’s face through the thatched bamboo window when I woke up, but did I really see it? I looked around for any sign of anyone, but all the houses were in darkness. I looked down the road and at first I saw nothing. Then I saw a flash of white cloth standing along the road. They were waiting. I jumped into the truck and drove to Yolli’s brother’s house, where her sister-in-law was making soft moans in pain.

They laid a mattress down in the bed of the truck. Yolli, Yeni, and Claudina (the neighbor and midwife), loaded Amalia into the back of the truck and got in with her. They told me I had to drive rapidly; the baby stopped moving.

I asked where to drive. At first they said Esquintla, a town I have never been to, but know generally where it is. Then they said, no, to the Port. I had just been there during the day to buy supplies for our small surf hotel in this remote location. I love walking the open air market and haggling for fresh fruits and vegetables.

I asked if any of the women wanted to get into the cab, but they all declined. They needed to be there to help. I closed the door and started to race down the long dirt road. I look at the clock. I expected it to be early in the morning, but it was midnight, exactly. I had only slept for an hour or two.

The waning half-moon was coming up orange like a mandarin slice. It hovered above the road I barreled down. I felt like I was surfing the road. My headlights were revealing the dark layers of bumps and dips along the sandy road. I was turning, pumping, floating. I drove as fast as I could, but wasn’t sure. Was it too rough in the back of the truck? Should I be going even faster? I was still awakening. I knew the tide was low; the moon was pulling at the water to the east; the moon was calling to us. Was it helping us, luring us towards it, pulling the baby? I was trying to drive faster. It was quiet and alone in the cab of the truck.

When we hit pavement, I raced even faster along the winding asphalt roads searching with as much accuracy as I could for the breaks and washouts. I had just driven it, and most of the washouts I remember and slow down. I can feel the thrum of the engine as it accelerates out from slow to fast. I am shifting down and up like a racecar driver. In small urban areas, there are massive speed bumps. Erin has a bruise darker then the night on her leg from a van ride. The driver didn’t see the bump coming and hit it with full force. Everyone went flying in the bus; Erin came crashing down onto the metal bar of the seat in front of her.

I imagined Amalia lifting to flight out of the truck; I imagined the ladies soaring as they hold each others hands, and the baby leaving the water womb and entering a world ordered by chaos. I feared death. Still, I pushed 80, then 100, then 120 kilometers an hour, downshifting for each speed bump, slowly pulling over the mound, letting the back of the truck slide off, and then accelerating back to speed in seconds. I tried to look in the mirrors for what was happening. I couldn’t hear screams, I couldn’t hear moans, I couldn’t hear the ocean thundering waves, or the tide slowly rising. I leaned toward the window and raced.

When we reached the port, I heard Yolli bang on the roof of the truck. My hearts was sinking, the swell diminishing. I pulled over and she jumped inside to direct me. We drove dark streets in the night looking for the hospital. She said she knew where it was. “Aca,” here, she hollered. It took me a while to understand aca as here. I was taught, aqui. They told me once that aca was a Mexican use of the word, but I had never heard it before.

We pull down the road and I expect bright lights, signs, but there is only darkness. She looks at each building as a possibility, but it is one I can’t fathom. There are no bright-lit signs, no sirens to come to rescue. Down this dark alley is a hospital. No. We begin to zig-zag, but they all look the same to me. We stop on the main road and Yolli jumps out of the truck. She says, wait here, in Spanish and I watch as she runs down the block. She has a cold, el gripe, and I know she shouldn’t be running or up at this hour. She disappears off the street and into darkness. I pull the truck up to where she ran from the road. She emerges from a house with a lady following her and a guy walking his motorcycle out from the house. The lady jumps in, and we follow the motorcycle. I don’t know who she is, or who he is, or how Yolli knew them, if she knew them. I didn’t understand how she would know where a person lives in this chaos of darkness and alleys, but not know where the hospital was.

The motorcycle driver weaves down the road, turns down an alley that looks the same as all the rest, and ends at a dark parking lot of a blue building. There are no lights on. Yolli runs to the door and tries it. It is closed. A security guard comes out and I can understand when he says go to the firemen. Bomberos. We pile back into the car and light off after the motorcycle heading to the fire station.

At the station, the lights are also off. Yolli and her friend run to the door and call for help. The large fire truck has a spider cracked front window; the ambulance is old and dirty. A light comes on from inside and I run back to the truck. Amalia is wailing softly, but it is growing. The midwife yells for me to help. Yeni is behind Amalia and she yells for me to come, quick, help, but I don’t understand what they want. The Spanish is too quick, too emotional. I run for Yolli. I tell them to come quick. I run back to the truck and I hear it. It is a sound as soothing as ocean waves. I hear the soft cries of a newly born baby. It is a girl. I holler out to everyone in the only words I can find for the moment—“there is a baby.”

The expression changes on faces like winds shift with tides. The tide must have turned. The moon is moving above us. Yeni makes fun of me. She said, they wanted me to help, and I ran away. They said they needed me to help push on her belly. They all are happy and everyone is smiles. The laughs at my sake are welcome. I can feel the pull of the moon; it makes us all lighter for a moment.

The firemen emerge. They assist the midwife as she cuts the umbilical cord. Yolli takes the little girl into the fire station to clean her a little and put on some clothes. I watch as she carefully wraps her up. Her skin is pale and they joke with me that I must be the father. Her lips are rosy. Her eyes are dark. They hand her back to the mother for a second. Yolli tells me they won’t name her for another month. The baby looks up from the back of a pickup truck at Orion, the hunter moving across the winter sky. The moon is in her eyes.

Amalia never gets out of the pick-up truck. We try to cover her up to keep her warm. Yolli takes the baby into the cab with me. Yeni and Claudina stay in the back as we drive real slowly back to the beach. Yeni is having a cigarette and I can see the orange glow. Yolli and I talk the whole slow drive back as best we can in this world of difficult translations. The baby cries softly. We talk about kids. Yolli is younger than me, but has three kids, and doesn’t want anymore. She asks me why I don’t have any. I don’t have a good reply—not one that translates well, not for me, or for her. Besides, life is the translation. I could say that I am not ready, or that Erin is not ready, or even that the world is not ready. I simply say, I don’t know. It is more convoluted then I try to explain. It is simple to say; however, I think about destiny, about the forces outside of our power. The moon is always pulling and pushing. The wind is calling. The road is filled with potholes and tumulos. The ocean is tumultuous.

We pull into the village around four in the morning. I help carry Amalia into her house and into bed. The house is simple, the floor dirty; they have no glass windows, no locking doors with brass hinges or peep holes and door bells. We carry her to the bed and everyone thanks me. Everyone is happy. Yeni is outside trying to clean up some of the blood from the back of the pick-up truck. I tell her to leave it for now. It doesn’t matter. I drive to the hotel, turn off the lights and head to bed. I crawl under the mosquito net and push my body against Erin’s warm skin. I can hear the ocean.

In the morning, Yille tells me there was a death in the village last night, a neighbor boy, the son of the man who built the main building of the hotel. The boy had a mental disability. Every day I would see him walk up to the fence and stare in at us. He looked trapped in his body and mind. Before the fence was built, he would come into the middle of the property and just stand there. He always seemed to be heading towards the beach. I think the tide called to him. Yille explains his death as a part of the moon. He says he was very weak and people who are weak are more affected by the moon. Their whole body feels the moon pulling at them. He said the boy took a few breaths, and just stopped. Life, as a circle, is too neat and clichéd. No, it is amoeba-like, gelatinous, and squirmy. Sometimes it looks like a wave about to crash.

When you sit on your surfboard on the edge of a large ocean and look out, it is hard to imagine the circle. You look out into an abyss; the ocean falls off at the horizon, and the sky is too empty and too far away. The tides change every 6 hours, the earth rotates every 24 hours, the moon orbits every 27 days, the earth orbits the sun every 365 days, and we all wonder how many times we will get to watch it.
