## The problem

You go to meet a community partner. You have forty-five minutes, three things to cover, and a program that genuinely helps people. You arrive on time, explain it clearly, answer questions honestly, and leave with an agreement to follow up. By every visible measure the meeting went well.

Six months later the partnership has not gone anywhere, and nobody can say why.

What happened is that the meeting was a transaction. The partner learned what we offer. We learned nothing about them except whether they said yes.

> &ldquo;We do not need another program. We need someone who will listen long enough to understand why the last three did not stick.&rdquo;

A transactional conversation asks *what can we deliver*. A transformational one asks *how do we walk alongside each other*. The difference is not warmth. Plenty of transactional meetings are friendly. The difference is who is leading, and whether anything the other person said changed what happens next. **The test:** after the conversation, can you say something about that person that you could not have found in a document?

## The one thing to know

Five steps. The order matters more than the wording, and almost all the difficulty is in the first three.

<FrameworkGrid>

<FrameworkItem title={"Start with purpose"}>
Say out loud why you wanted the conversation, and make it about learning rather than about what you need. "I would like to understand what matters most to you in your work lately."
</FrameworkItem>

<FrameworkItem title={"Explore their story"}>
Let them lead. Follow what they choose to talk about rather than steering back to your agenda. If you are steering, you have left the flow.
</FrameworkItem>

<FrameworkItem title={"Reflect what you hear"}>
Say back the meaning, not the facts. "It sounds like being taken seriously matters more to you than the funding does." This is the step people skip, and it is the one that builds trust.
</FrameworkItem>

<FrameworkItem title={"Look for shared interest"}>
Find the genuine overlap between what they need and what you are there for. Genuine is the word that matters. A manufactured overlap is worse than an honest gap.
</FrameworkItem>

<FrameworkItem title={"Agree on one next step"}>
One small thing you both do. One, not five.
</FrameworkItem>

</FrameworkGrid>

Steps 4 and 5 are usually easy once 1 to 3 have actually happened, and impossible when they have not.

## Four things to try this week

Pick one. Not four.

<PracticeGrid>

<Practice title={"The learn or influence check"} time={"60 seconds, before your next meeting"} finding={"Influence is not a shameful answer. But decide it deliberately, and consider whether five minutes of learning first would change what you are trying to influence."}>
Pause and ask yourself one question: am I trying to learn, or am I trying to influence?
</Practice>

<Practice title={"Ten percent slower"} time={"One conversation"} finding={"What usually happens is that they keep talking, and the second thing they say is more useful than the first."}>
When the other person finishes speaking, count to three before you respond. That is the whole exercise.
</Practice>

<Practice title={"The 70/30 rule"} time={"One coffee or lunch"} finding={"Most people find this exhausting the first time. That is useful information about how much of a normal conversation you spend preparing to speak."}>
Aim to spend seventy percent of the time listening and thirty talking. Use your thirty mostly on how and why questions.
</Practice>

<Practice title={"The two-minute debrief"} time={"After any conversation that mattered"} finding={"Nobody does this. It is the highest-return two minutes on the page."}>
Ask yourself three things: when did I want to jump in and fix something? What did I learn only because I slowed down? Do I understand what matters to them better than I did an hour ago?
</Practice>

</PracticeGrid>
