## The problem

A meeting goes well. People are polite, the agenda gets covered, a decision gets made, and nobody objects. Afterward two people who did not say much tell each other in the parking lot what they actually thought.

That meeting did not fail. It succeeded at the wrong thing. It converted a room full of knowledge into the opinion of whoever was most comfortable speaking.

Participation is never neutral. Who talks, who gets heard, and whose knowledge ends up shaping the decision are all influenced by power, history, title and how safe the room feels. Left alone, a meeting runs on whoever speaks first.

> Silence is not agreement. It is usually information you did not collect.

<Callout label="The shift">
From running a meeting well to designing who gets to shape it. A good facilitator keeps things moving. An inclusive one decides in advance how the quietest person in the room will get their idea onto the table, and notices in the moment when that is not happening.
</Callout>

## The one thing to know

Five moves. The first one happens before the meeting starts, which is the one people skip.

<FrameworkGrid>

<FrameworkItem title={"Design the invitation"}>
Decide before the meeting how people will be brought in. Round robin, written first then spoken, small groups before whole group, asking someone by name. Opening the floor generally is not a method; it is the absence of one.
</FrameworkItem>

<FrameworkItem title={"Track who is not speaking"}>
While you are running it. Not afterward. If you can name who has not spoken twenty minutes in, you can do something about it while it still matters.
</FrameworkItem>

<FrameworkItem title={"Interrupt early and kindly"}>
One person dominating is normal and it is your job, not theirs. Early and warm costs the relationship nothing. Late and blunt costs a lot.
</FrameworkItem>

<FrameworkItem title={"Ask for disagreement before you conclude"}>
Early agreement is often false agreement. "What is the case against this?" asked out loud, with a pause, gets you the objection that would otherwise arrive in the parking lot.
</FrameworkItem>

<FrameworkItem title={"Say what is actually open"}>
Tell people honestly which parts of the decision they can influence and which are already set. Consulting people on something already decided does more damage than not asking.
</FrameworkItem>

</FrameworkGrid>

<Callout label="The test" tone="clay">
Did anything in the meeting change because of someone who does not usually shape decisions?
</Callout>

## Four things to try this week

Pick one. Not four.

<PracticeGrid>

<Practice title={"Count the airtime"} time={"10 minutes, one meeting"} finding={"The distribution is almost always more skewed than anyone in the room believes, including you."}>
In your next meeting, keep a private tally of who speaks and roughly for how long. Do not intervene, just count.
</Practice>

<Practice title={"Write before you talk"} time={"One meeting"} finding={"This single change does more for participation than any amount of encouragement, because it removes the advantage of thinking fast out loud."}>
Before opening a discussion, ask everyone to write for sixty seconds. Then go round and take one line each.
</Practice>

<Practice title={"Ask for the objection"} time={"One decision"} finding={"The first ten seconds are uncomfortable. The answer usually arrives at about fifteen."}>
Before you confirm a decision, ask: "What is the strongest case against this?" Then wait through the silence.
</Practice>

<Practice title={"Name what is open"} time={"2 minutes, next time you consult anyone"} finding={"People forgive a narrow decision. They do not forgive discovering it was narrow after they invested in it."}>
Say out loud which parts of the decision are genuinely open and which are already fixed.
</Practice>

</PracticeGrid>
