Almost every plan starts with a need. A gap analysis, a risk index, a list of
what is missing. It is a reasonable place to start and it produces a reliable
result: a community described entirely by its problems, and a plan that only we
can deliver.

Meanwhile the barber who knows everyone, the church that already runs a food
line, the retired nurse three doors down, and the group chat that mobilised in
four hours during the last outage do not appear anywhere in the assessment.

They are the reason anything survives after we leave.

<Callout label="The shift">
From assessing a community to inventorying it. A needs assessment asks what is
wrong here. Asset mapping asks what is already working, who already has
standing, and what would keep working if we never came back.
</Callout>

## The one thing to know

<FrameworkGrid note="Four categories. Most people find the third one and stop, which is exactly the problem.">

<FrameworkItem title="Individuals">
The skills, knowledge and capacity of people who live there. The retired
teacher, the person who speaks three languages, the one everybody calls when
something goes wrong. Rarely on any list, and usually the most important column.
</FrameworkItem>

<FrameworkItem title="Associations">
Informal and voluntary groups. Block clubs, faith groups, sports leagues,
mutual aid networks, the group chat. No budget, no letterhead, real reach.
</FrameworkItem>

<FrameworkItem title="Institutions">
Schools, clinics, libraries, businesses, government offices. The easiest to
find, which is why maps that only have this column look complete and are not.
</FrameworkItem>

<FrameworkItem title="Physical and economic">
Land, buildings, transport, spaces people gather, money that circulates
locally. Where things happen matters as much as who makes them happen.
</FrameworkItem>

</FrameworkGrid>

<Callout label="The check" tone="clay">
If your map is mostly institutions, you have made a directory, not a map.
</Callout>

<PracticeGrid heading="Four things to try this week" note="Pick one. Not four.">

<Practice
  title="Fill the four corners"
  time="15 minutes, alone"
  finding="One box will be much thinner than the others. That is the finding, and it is usually Individuals or Associations."
>
Take one community you work in. Draw four boxes and fill each with as many
real, specific, named things as you can. Not categories — names.
</Practice>

<Practice
  title="Name five people with no title"
  time="10 minutes"
  finding="If you cannot get to five, that is not a fact about the community. It is a fact about how you have been engaging with it."
>
List five people in that community with real influence who hold no formal
position.
</Practice>

<Practice
  title="Ask the trust question"
  time="One conversation"
  finding="The answer is rarely the person on our contact list, and it is almost always worth the conversation."
>
Ask somebody who lives there: when something goes wrong here, who do people
actually go to?
</Practice>

<Practice
  title="Check your map against a resident"
  time="20 minutes"
  finding="This is the whole skill in miniature. Everything before it is a hypothesis."
>
Show your four boxes to one person who lives there and ask what is missing or
wrong.
</Practice>

</PracticeGrid>

<FacilitatorNote title="Running the one-hour version with a group">

The written module is deliberately solitary. The workshop exists because the
gap between an individual's map and a group's map *is* the teaching point —
people are confident about different columns, and the argument that produces is
more useful than any input you could give.

Structure that has worked: fifteen minutes filling the four corners
individually and in silence, twenty minutes merging onto one wall, and the rest
on a single question — what is on this map that most people do not know is on
this map?

Duplicates are information. The things named by three people independently are
the things that would actually get used in an emergency.

</FacilitatorNote>

<FacilitatorNote title="When the map comes back thin">

A thin map usually means one of three things, and they need different responses.

**The group is new to each other.** The map is accurate and the finding is the
point: the work is relational before it is logistical. Say so plainly.

**The group is answering the question they think you want.** Symptom:
everything on the map is an institution with a logo. Fix: ask for a specific
name of a specific person for each item, and watch what falls off.

**The area is genuinely under-resourced.** Resist filling the silence with
reassurance. Ask instead where people currently go *outside* the area, and how
they get there. That route is itself the asset.

</FacilitatorNote>

<FacilitatorNote title="A caution about extraction">

The failure mode of asset mapping is treating people's generosity as inventory.
An asset that has not consented is not an asset; it is an assumption.

If a group's map turns up a private business or an individual who is carrying
real load — the hardware store that opens up during heat events, the neighbour
with the truck — the next step is a conversation with that person, not an entry
on a list. Adding someone to an official roster they never agreed to join is
the fastest way to lose the most reliable thing on the map.

</FacilitatorNote>
