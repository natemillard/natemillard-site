## The problem

A neighborhood holds a preparedness workshop. Forty-seven people attend, thirty-one build a kit, and when something happens those thirty-one evacuate efficiently and reunite with their families. That is the program working, and it is worth doing.

Meanwhile three neighbors who attended nothing check on the elderly residents on their street, get two families out, and open their homes to a fourth.

The second thing happened because those neighbors already knew each other. The trust existed before the crisis and no program created it. Most of what determines whether a community gets through something is like that: it sits at a level our programs do not touch.

> A program can succeed completely and change nothing about whether the neighborhood is more resilient.

<Callout label="The shift">
From delivering a program to designing across levels. Behavior and resilience live at five nested levels at once. A workshop that only operates at one of them will produce exactly the result it was designed for, and no more.
</Callout>

## The one thing to know

Five levels. Almost every program we run operates at one or two, and the interesting work is at the joins.

<FrameworkGrid>

<FrameworkItem title={"Individual"}>
What one person knows, believes and can do. Build a kit, learn the plan, install the alarm. Where most of our programs live.
</FrameworkItem>

<FrameworkItem title={"Interpersonal"}>
The relationships between people. Whether neighbors know each other well enough to knock on a door at midnight. Almost never designed for, and frequently decisive.
</FrameworkItem>

<FrameworkItem title={"Organizational"}>
Schools, employers, faith groups, clinics. Institutions that can carry a practice further than we can and keep carrying it after we leave.
</FrameworkItem>

<FrameworkItem title={"Community"}>
Norms, shared expectations, what people around here do. Slow to shift and durable once shifted.
</FrameworkItem>

<FrameworkItem title={"Systems"}>
Policy, funding, codes, rules. The level people assume is above their pay grade, which is sometimes true and often not.
</FrameworkItem>

</FrameworkGrid>

<Callout label="The design question" tone="clay">
When you plan something at one level, what is it doing to the others? Everything you do moves more than the level you aimed at, whether or not you can see it.
</Callout>

## Four things to try this week

Pick one. Not four.

<PracticeGrid>

<Practice title={"Map one program"} time={"15 minutes, alone"} finding={"Most programs come out strong at Individual and empty at Interpersonal and Systems. That pattern is the finding."}>
Take a program you run. Write what it does at each of the five levels. Mark each one strong, present or absent.
</Practice>

<Practice title={"Trace a ripple"} time={"10 minutes"} finding={"The unintended effects are usually at the level you were not designing for."}>
Pick a decision you made recently and trace it forward through the levels. Who else does it touch? What norm does it reinforce?
</Practice>

<Practice title={"Find the missing partner"} time={"10 minutes"} finding={"There is almost always someone. The organizational and systems levels are full of people who would work with us and have never been asked."}>
Look at your emptiest level and ask who already operates there.
</Practice>

<Practice title={"Ask the sustainability question"} time={"5 minutes"} finding={"Whatever survives is operating above the individual level. Whatever stops was only ever a program."}>
If your chapter stopped this program tomorrow, what would keep going and why?
</Practice>

</PracticeGrid>
