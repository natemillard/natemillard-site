Every community already has an emergency response. It is simply not written
down anywhere. It is the woman on the corner who has everyone's phone number,
the hardware store that opens after hours, the church van, the retired nurse,
the group chat that formed during the last outage and never quite dissolved.

The premise of this session is that most preparedness work starts in the wrong
place — with a plan for what a community *should* have — when it could start
with an inventory of what a community already does. That inventory is faster to
build, more accurate, and it belongs to the people in the room.

<ScenarioCard title="A Thursday in August" setting="A town of 4,000, two hours from the nearest city" time="Read aloud, 3 minutes">

The power has been out for eleven hours. It is 104 degrees. The county has
announced a cooling centre at the fairgrounds, four miles outside town, and put
it on a website nobody can load.

By late afternoon, sixty people are sitting in the public library, which has a
generator and did not announce anything. Someone propped the door open. Someone
else drove twice to collect people who could not walk it.

Nobody planned this. It worked anyway.

</ScenarioCard>

<ReflectionPrompt
  question="In your own community, where would the sixty people have gone?"
  notes
  time="4 minutes, alone"
>

Not where you would *want* them to go. Where they would actually go, based on
what has happened before.

</ReflectionPrompt>

## Why the official map is usually wrong

The formal map — shelters, agencies, hotlines — describes a system's intentions.
The real map describes people's habits. The two overlap less than planners
expect, and the gap is where preparedness efforts quietly fail.

## The walk

The core of the session is not a discussion. It is a walk, on foot, in pairs,
through an area the group knows.

<StepSequence title="Running the session">

<Step title="Open with the scenario" time="10 min">
Read the scenario aloud. Ask the reflection question. Do not collect answers
yet — let people hold their own answer while they walk.
</Step>

<Step title="Name the categories" time="10 min">
Introduce four kinds of asset: **places** people already gather, **people**
others turn to, **things** that get shared, and **channels** that carry
information. Keep it to four; more categories produce worse maps.
</Step>

<Step title="Walk in pairs" time="35 min">
Pairs take one category each and walk a defined block or route. They write down
what they see and who they know. The walking matters — sitting in a room
produces a list of institutions, walking produces a list of specifics.
</Step>

<Step title="Build the shared map" time="20 min">
Back in the room, pairs add what they found to a single large sheet or wall.
Duplicates are useful information: the things named three times are the things
that would actually be used.
</Step>

<Step title="Find one gap" time="15 min">
Ask a single question: what is on this map that most people do not know is on
this map? That gap — a known resource with an unknown route to it — is almost
always more actionable than an absent resource.
</Step>

</StepSequence>

<FacilitatorNote title="What to do when the map comes back thin">

A thin map usually means one of three things, and they need different responses.

**The group is new to each other.** Then the map is accurate and the finding is
the point: the work is relational before it is logistical. Say so plainly.

**The group is answering the question they think you want.** Symptom: everything
on the map is an institution with a logo. Fix: ask for a specific name of a
specific person for each item, and watch what falls off.

**The area you sent them to is genuinely under-resourced.** Then resist the urge
to fill the silence with reassurance. Ask instead where people currently go
*outside* the area, and how they get there. That route is itself the asset.

</FacilitatorNote>

<FacilitatorNote title="Handling the person who wants to talk about the county">

There is often one participant who redirects everything toward what the county
or the agency should be doing. This is usually a legitimate grievance and a
poor use of ninety minutes.

Acknowledge it once, concretely, and write it on a separate sheet labelled
*things to take upward*. That sheet should be real — collect it and act on it —
which is what makes the redirect work rather than feel like a dismissal.

</FacilitatorNote>

## A decision the group will face

Late in most sessions a version of this comes up, and how it gets answered
shapes what the group does next.

    { label: 'Send two people to talk to him and ask what would help',
      judgement: 'sound',
      outcome: 'Slow, and it may produce nothing formal. But it treats the asset as a relationship, which is what it is — and the conversation itself surfaces what he actually needs, which is usually smaller and more specific than anyone assumed.' },
    { label: 'Leave it off the map to protect him',
      judgement: 'mixed',
      outcome: 'Protective, and it keeps the group honest about consent. But it also means the group is now planning around a resource it has decided not to discuss, which tends to produce plans that quietly depend on luck.' },
  ]}
/>

<RevealCard prompt="Why is the second option usually right, even when it produces nothing formal?" label="One reading">

Because the failure mode of asset mapping is extraction — treating people's
generosity as inventory. An asset that has not consented is not an asset; it is
an assumption. The conversation converts one into the other, and it is the only
step that does.

It also changes who the group is to that person. Next time, they call.

</RevealCard>

## Closing

<ReflectionPrompt question="What is one thing on your map that you personally could make one degree easier to find?" notes time="5 minutes">

Not a plan. One degree. A phone number written somewhere, an introduction made,
a door someone knows to knock on.

</ReflectionPrompt>

<FacilitatorNote title="Closing the session without deflating it">

Resist ending on commitments. Ninety minutes produces energy and a map; it does
not produce capacity, and asking for pledges at the end tends to convert the
energy into guilt.

End instead by naming what the group now knows that it did not know at 9am, and
by saying when the map will be looked at again. A date is worth more than a
promise.

</FacilitatorNote>
