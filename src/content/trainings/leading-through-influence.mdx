## The problem

Almost everything that matters in community work happens across a boundary. Other departments, other organizations, volunteers, residents, partners. None of them work for you, and none of them can be told.

The default response when authority is missing is to escalate, wait, or work around. All three are expensive. Escalating burns the relationship, waiting means it does not happen, and working around means doing it yourself, which is the reason so many good people are so tired.

The alternative is not persuasion. It is understanding what the other person is trying to protect, and building a route where the thing you want and the thing they need are the same move.

> A decision that stalls is rarely a disagreement about the answer. It is usually a disagreement nobody has said out loud.

<Callout label="The shift">
From asking how do I convince them to asking what would have to be true for them to want this. Those produce completely different conversations, and only one of them survives contact with someone who has their own board to answer to.
</Callout>

## The one thing to know

Three kinds of influence. Most people can name the first and act as though it is the only one.

<FrameworkGrid>

<FrameworkItem title={"Formal"}>
Title, budget, seat at the table, signing authority. Easy to see, easy to overrate. Plenty of people with formal authority cannot make anything happen.
</FrameworkItem>

<FrameworkItem title={"Informal"}>
Who people actually listen to. The person who has been there twenty years, the one everybody calls first, the one whose quiet doubt kills a proposal. Rarely on the org chart, frequently decisive.
</FrameworkItem>

<FrameworkItem title={"Moral"}>
Standing that comes from lived experience or from having been right before. The resident who was there during the last flood. The partner who warned everyone about the thing that then happened. Hard to argue with and easy to leave out of the room.
</FrameworkItem>

</FrameworkGrid>

**Map all three before you act, including the people who will resist.** Most failed influence attempts are well-argued cases delivered to the wrong person in the wrong order.

## Four things to try this week

Pick one. Not four.

<PracticeGrid>

<Practice title={"Map before you ask"} time={"10 minutes, one live decision"} finding={"The order you talk to people in matters more than what you say to any one of them."}>
List everyone who touches it. Mark each as formal, informal or moral influence, and mark who will resist.
</Practice>

<Practice title={"Find what they are protecting"} time={"5 minutes"} finding={"Reputation, their team's workload, a promise they already made. It is almost never the thing they are arguing about."}>
Pick the person most likely to block you and write down what they are trying to protect. Not what they want, what they are protecting.
</Practice>

<Practice title={"Do the pre-meeting"} time={"One conversation"} finding={"Alignment built in the meeting is fragile. Alignment built before it holds, and the meeting becomes a confirmation rather than a contest."}>
Before the meeting where a decision gets made, talk to one person who will be in it.
</Practice>

<Practice title={"Tell the truth about the tradeoff"} time={"2 minutes"} finding={"Saying it first removes their strongest objection and buys more credibility than any amount of framing."}>
Name out loud what your proposal costs the other party.
</Practice>

</PracticeGrid>
