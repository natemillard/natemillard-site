## The problem

You walk into a community meeting. Before you say anything, the room has already read your title, your organization, your age, how you are dressed, and whether you look like the people who usually show up and then leave.

You did not choose most of that and you cannot put it down. What you can do is know what it is, because the alternative is being the only person in the room who does not.

This is the skill underneath the other twelve. Every one of them asks you to notice something: who is not talking, what a partner actually needs, whether a program is really community-led. None of that noticing is reliable if you cannot see your own defaults first.

> The question is not whether you bring yourself into the room. It is whether you know what you brought.

<Callout label="The shift">
From thinking about self-awareness as a personality trait to treating it as a working practice. Not who am I, which is a large question with no end. But narrower ones: what do I do under pressure, what kind of person do I find hardest to hear, and what does this room see when I arrive?
</Callout>

## The one thing to know

Three questions. They take five minutes and they are worth asking before anything that matters.

<FrameworkGrid>

<FrameworkItem title={"What am I bringing?"}>
Your role, your history with this community, your organization's history with it, what you need from this meeting, and what mood you arrived in. All of it is in the room whether you name it or not.
</FrameworkItem>

<FrameworkItem title={"What do I default to?"}>
Under time pressure, disagreement or silence, everyone has a move. Some people fill the space. Some go quiet and decide later that the group agreed. Some get more certain the less certain they feel. Yours is predictable to everyone but you.
</FrameworkItem>

<FrameworkItem title={"Whose voice is hardest for me to hear?"}>
Not who do I disagree with. Who do I quietly discount. The person who talks too long, the one who is angry, the one who repeats themselves, the one who is junior. There is always someone, and they are often the one holding the information you need.
</FrameworkItem>

</FrameworkGrid>

<Callout label="The uncomfortable part" tone="clay">
The third question is the one that changes anything, and it is the one people answer last.
</Callout>

## Four things to try this week

Pick one. Not four.

<PracticeGrid>

<Practice title={"The arrival check"} time={"60 seconds, before your next meeting"} finding={"You are not trying to fix any of it. Naming it is enough to stop it running the meeting for you."}>
Before you walk in, name three things: what you need from this, what mood you are actually in, and what this group already thinks about your organization.
</Practice>

<Practice title={"Find your default"} time={"10 minutes, once"} finding={"Most people find the same move three times. That is your default, and it is the thing colleagues could have told you years ago."}>
Think of the last three times a conversation got tense. Write down what you did each time. Not what you felt, what you did.
</Practice>

<Practice title={"Name the hard voice"} time={"5 minutes"} finding={"The reason is usually about style rather than substance. That is worth knowing before their information turns out to matter."}>
Write down one person whose contributions you find yourself discounting. Then write why, honestly.
</Practice>

<Practice title={"Ask one person"} time={"15 minutes, harder than it sounds"} finding={"This is the only item on the page that gives you information you cannot generate alone, which is why it is worth the discomfort."}>
Ask a colleague who has seen you facilitate: what do I do when a room goes quiet? Then do not explain or defend the answer.
</Practice>

</PracticeGrid>
