Seven skills are load-bearing here, more than in any other phase. That is not
an accident of how the phase was written. Phase 1 covers people, programs,
partnerships and a seven-theme self-assessment, so it touches nearly
everything, at the lowest stakes you will ever get.

## Phase 1 is the rehearsal for Phase 3

This is the argument worth reading twice.

In Community Discovery you will sit with people who have no particular reason
to trust you, in a place where your assumptions are the largest risk in the
room, and nobody there will correct you politely. Every one of those conditions
has a low-stakes version inside your own chapter. That is what Phase 1 is for.
The map is the byproduct.

Five of the six load-bearing skills in Phase 3 are also load-bearing here. The
sixth, Building Cross-Sector Partnerships, is the one thing Community Discovery
asks for that Phase 1 cannot rehearse for you. Everything else, you can
practice first on people who already know you.

## Eight tasks, eight reps

Everything below is something you have to do anyway. The only change is doing
it on purpose, naming the skill while you do it, and knowing what it is
preparing you for.

### Mapping your own people, claim then add

**Skills:** Self-Discovery, Building Trust with Others.
**Rehearses:** Phase 3, where you sit with residents and your own assumptions
are the biggest risk in the room.
**Make it a real rep:** You go first, and name one thing you are not good at
before you ask anybody else to. The room calibrates to whatever you model in
the first ninety seconds.

### The one-to-ones between sessions

**Skill:** Building Trust with Others.
**Rehearses:** Phase 3, which is almost entirely one-to-ones with people who
have no reason to trust you yet.
**Make it a real rep:** Do at least four before Phase 1 ends, with team members
you know least well. Ask about them rather than about their function. Do not
take notes in the room; write them afterward.

### The volunteer pipeline numbers, and the "how do we know" column

**Skill:** Data-Informed Learning.
**Rehearses:** Phase 2, where you have to defend a priority area choice with
data in front of people who were not in the room.
**Make it a real rep:** Write the source beside every number. Where there is no
source, write "assumption" rather than leaving it blank. Count how many say
assumption. That count is the finding.

### Programs: where we are not

**Skills:** Asset Mapping, Prioritization.
**Rehearses:** Phase 2, where choosing one area means saying no somewhere you
currently show up.
**Make it a real rep:** List the gaps before you explain them. Explaining comes
first for most teams, and it is how a gap stops being a gap and becomes a
reason.

### Partnerships: reach, and who holds each one

**Skill:** Stakeholder Mapping.
**Rehearses:** Phase 3, where you need to know who actually holds influence
rather than who is on a contact list.
**Make it a real rep:** Beside every partner write the one person who holds it,
and what happens if that person leaves. The partnerships nobody can name a
holder for are the ones you thought you had.

### Partnerships: transactional or two-way

**Skill:** Aligning Our Priorities with Partners.
**Rehearses:** Phase 4, where a plan built on what we want from partners does
not survive contact with them.
**Make it a real rep:** For each partner, write what they get. One line,
concrete. Where you cannot write it, that is the honest state of the
relationship and it belongs on the page.

### Running the recurring hour itself

**Skills:** Running Effective Meetings, Inclusive Facilitation.
**Rehearses:** Phase 3, where you convene a room in somebody else's
neighborhood and you are not the expert in it.
**Make it a real rep:** Rotate who facilitates. Have the scribe note who spoke
and who did not, and read it back at the end of the session. It is
uncomfortable exactly once.

### The maturity self-assessment

**Skills:** Inclusive Facilitation, Leading Through Influence.
**Rehearses:** Phase 4, where you ask functional leads to align to a plan you
do not control.
**Make it a real rep:** Before the group votes on a rating, have the person who
would rate it lowest speak first. Ratings converge toward whoever speaks first,
so choose who that is.

## One skill per session

Do not try to practice seven skills at once. Name one skill at the top of each
session, practice it deliberately for that hour, and name it again at the
close: what did we do differently, and what happened. This is the single change
that turns Phase 1 from data collection into training.

A workable sequence, at your own pace, repeating any session that did not land:

1. Set-up: purpose, ground rules, who is in the room and why. *Running Effective Meetings*
2. People, part one: claim then add. *Self-Discovery*
3. People, part two: pipeline, roles, where people are. *Data-Informed Learning*
4. Programs: where we show up, and where we do not. *Asset Mapping*
5. Partnerships, part one: reach, and who holds each. *Stakeholder Mapping*
6. Partnerships, part two: depth, and what they get. *Aligning Our Priorities with Partners*
7. Maturity themes, as many sessions as it takes. *Inclusive Facilitation*
8. Synthesis: what we have, what we are missing, what is realistic. *Prioritization*

Running underneath all of it, and never a session of its own: Building Trust
with Others, practiced in the one-to-ones you hold between sessions. It is the
skill Phase 3 will lean on hardest and the only one on this list you cannot
practice in a group.

## How to tell it is working

- Somebody other than you can name the skill the session was practicing, unprompted, a week later.
- At least one person has changed how they do something outside the session because of it.
- The "how do we know" answers are getting more honest over time rather than more polished.
- People are saying things in the room that they would previously have said to you afterward in the hallway.
- You are less certain about your chapter than you were in session one, and more specific.

If none of these are true after four sessions, the sessions are being run as
status meetings. That is the most common failure and it is recoverable: pick
one skill, name it out loud, and run the next hour differently.
